Understanding Title Work

The agreement needs one person on title who passes credit. Not every owner, just one who qualifies. Confirm who's on title from your phone before you knock, and don't leave the home without the documents that prove your signer can sign. A missing one doesn't slow the deal down. It stops it.

Deeds, Owners, and the Words That Matter

You don't need to be a title attorney. Recognize a few terms and you can read a property record and know who signs.

How ownership transfers

  • Grantor gives, grantee gets, through a deed.
  • Warranty Deed: the strong one. Seller guarantees clear ownership. Most home purchases.
  • Quitclaim Deed: transfers whatever interest exists, no guarantees. Common in family, divorce, and Trust/LLC moves. Look closer.

Owner vs. borrower

  • The note is the loan; the mortgage is recorded against the property.
  • All owners are on the mortgage, but not all owners are borrowers.
  • Title decides who can sign, not the loan. The required title-signer has to be on the deed, not just the mortgage.

Where the record lives

  • Deeds get recorded with the town clerk.
  • Each gets a Book and Page number, the document's address.
  • Anyone can pull it, so ownership is something you verify, not just ask about.

AnswerOwnership likely changed outside a normal sale: divorce, family transfer, or a move into a Trust/LLC. Confirm who's on title now before assuming.

Confirm Ownership From Your Phone

The most useful habit in this guide. Two minutes before an appointment, look the property up. Google the town name plus any source below.

  1. Vision Appraisal

    The assessor's database. Owner name, property details, sale history. Fastest first look.

    "[Town] Vision Appraisal"

  2. Town Clerk

    The land records. Deeds, mortgages, Book/Page numbers. Confirm the deed and how it's held.

    "[Town] Town Clerk"

  3. My Tax Bill

    Who's taxed for it. Quick cross-check on the name and that taxes are current.

    "[Town] My Tax Bill"

  4. Town GIS

    The mapping system. Owner, parcel, satellite view. Confirm the right home before you go.

    "[Town] GIS"
The two-minute habit: note every name on title and check whether the owner is a person or an entity ("Smith Family Trust" or "123 Main LLC"). Now you walk in knowing who signs.
Pick a real upcoming appointment. Look it up live right now. Who's on title?
Name two sources to confirm an owner, and what each adds.

AnswerAny two: Vision Appraisal (fast owner + history), Town Clerk (the deed + Book/Page), My Tax Bill (name cross-check), Town GIS (parcel + confirm the right house).

When the Home Is Held in an LLC

The owner on title is a company, not a person. The deed alone isn't enough. You need proof of who can sign for the company, it isn't at the town, and the build can't start until you have it.

What's different

  • The deed shows the LLC owns the home, not the people living there.
  • A person with authority signs, usually a managing member.
  • Proof isn't recorded at the town. It's in the formation papers.

What you collect

  • The deed showing the LLC owns the home.
  • Formation documents (Articles of Organization, operating agreement) showing who's authorized.
  • From the homeowner, not the clerk. Ask before the appointment, and leave with it that night.
Rep · asking for the docs"Looks like the home's in an LLC, common with family and rental property. Before we sit down I'll need the formation paperwork that names who's authorized to sign. Can you have it out on the table? It's the piece that decides how fast we can start."

When the Home Is Held in a Trust

Common with estate planning. The Trust owns the home, and the Certificate of Trust is what proves who can sign for it. No certificate, no validated signer, no Notice to Proceed. The job stops.

What's different

  • The deed names a trust, often the "Smith Family Trust."
  • The trustee signs. Often the homeowner in front of you, but confirm it.
  • Trust documents aren't recorded at the town. Only the homeowner can hand them to you.

What you collect

  • List the trustee on the agreement, not the trust itself.
  • Get the Certificate of Trust before you leave. It is the specific document LightReach needs, and the agreement can't be validated without it.
  • Bonus with LightReach: someone other than the trustee can be the credit-passer.
Rep · asking for the docs"I see the home's in a family trust, smart planning. One thing I need before I head out: a copy of the Certificate of Trust. It's the page that shows you're the trustee who can sign, and your install can't be scheduled without it. Where do you keep the trust folder?"

AnswerThe named trustee signs, and you need a Certificate of Trust (list the trustee, not the trust). An LLC needs an authorized member plus formation papers. Both come from the homeowner, not the town, and both come home with you the same night. Leave without one and the job can't get NTP.

Pre-appointment title checklist

  • Looked up the property; noted every name on title.
  • Confirmed owner is a person or an entity.
  • If an entity, asked for the formation or trust documents so they are on the table when you arrive.
  • Confirmed at least one title-holder will run credit (add an immediate-family co-applicant if you need more strength).
  • Checked for recent changes: Quitclaim, divorce, passing, sale.
  • Condo/HOA? Confirmation form ready.
  • Flagged anything unusual to the title team.

Before you leave the home

  • The signer on the agreement is the name that holds authority on title.
  • Entity-held? The Certificate of Trust or formation documents are in your hand or already in your inbox.
  • Condo or HOA? Confirmation of Ownership and Access form signed.
  • Anything you could not resolve goes to the title team tonight, with the homeowner still reachable.
Validate at the table. Every document you walk out without is one you chase by phone against a homeowner who has already moved on to the rest of their week. You are in their living room once. Ask while you are in it.
